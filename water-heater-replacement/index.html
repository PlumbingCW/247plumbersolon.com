A failed tank on a Sunday morning or a weeknight

A tank that leaks from its body cannot be patched, and the leak only grows. Left alone, it turns into a flooded utility room. After-hours replacement is possible, and we aim to set the new heater the same day when stock and access allow it.

Read the data label on the side of the tank before you call. Fuel type, gallon size, and model number let us line up a matching unit before the truck leaves. A phone photo of the label and the pipes above the tank helps even more.

Same style of tank, or a different kind of heater

Keep the tank style

  • Usually the shortest job because pipes, venting, and wiring already match
  • Controls and routines stay familiar
  • Best fit for a midnight failure when hot water is the only goal
  • Smaller chance of surprises behind the walls

Switch the type

  • Tankless frees floor space but needs a gas line and venting check
  • Heat pump tanks pull heat from room air and need open clearance and a drain
  • A fuel change can mean a new gas line or a new circuit
  • Plan these as scheduled work, not a late-night swap
Local note

Solon asks for a permit on this job

Solon's Required Residential Permits Guide lists replacing a water heater among the plumbing work that needs a permit. Swapping a fixture does not. Contractors working in the city also register with Solon each year and show a current Ohio plumbing license.

Ask any installer who handles the permit and who calls in the inspection before you agree to the job. An unpermitted swap can raise questions later, so settle it up front. The Solon Building Department can confirm current forms and steps.

From shutoff to the first hot tap

  1. 1

    Drain and disconnect

    We stop the water and the gas or power, drain the old tank to a floor drain or outdoors, and disconnect the lines.

  2. 2

    The heater goes on a level spot, in a drain pan where space allows.

  3. 3

Warning signs before a tank gives out

A date well into its second decade

The serial number usually encodes the build date. A tank that old is on borrowed time even if it still heats.

Rust at the base or around fittings

Orange stains or flaking metal mean the shell is attacking itself from outside or inside.

Moisture that returns after wiping

A damp floor that keeps coming back usually means a slow seep, not a spill.

Loud popping or rumbling

A heavy sediment layer hurts efficiency and stresses the bottom of the steel.

Hot water that fades sooner each month

Scale on elements and a crusted tank bottom shrink usable supply over time.
