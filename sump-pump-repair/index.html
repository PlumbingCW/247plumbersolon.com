The urgent case

The pit is full and the pump will not run

A sump pump has one job, which is to move water out of the pit before it reaches your floor. When it stops, the pit fills in minutes during a heavy storm, and then the basement does. The usual causes are a stuck float switch, a burned-out motor, a clogged intake or a power loss.

Check the simple things while you wait. Look at the breaker and the outlet, and confirm the cord is still plugged in. If you can see the pit clearly and the floor around it is dry, lift the float by hand to see whether the pump starts. Do not reach in if any water is near a live cord.

Before we arrive: protect the room and yourself

  • ✓Do not stand in water near outlets.Standing water plus power is the real danger, so shut off the breaker for that area if you can reach it dry.
  • ✓Lift stored items.Cardboard boxes, rugs and appliance cords on the floor soak up water first.
  • ✓Bucket and mop as a stopgap.A wet vacuum rated for water can slow a small leak, but it does not replace a working pump.
  • ✓Shut off the washer and dishwasher.Their drain water often feeds the pit, so skip them until the pump is running.
  • ✓Tell us what you hear.A humming pump that will not pump, a pump that runs without moving water, and total silence each point to different fixes.

Not every failed pump needs to be thrown out. This is how plumbers usually sort it.

If the pump is many years old, replacing it often makes more sense than a second repair. We show you the failed part before you decide.

Warning signs before a pump quits

Loud grinding or rattling

A worn bearing or debris in the impeller often sounds like this days before total failure.

Running when it has not rained

Constant cycling may mean a bad float, or groundwater and a leaking downspout feeding the pit.

Rust or orange water in the pit

Rusty water in the pit can mean corroding metal parts, so the pump housing deserves a look.

Pump that vibrates or rocks

Loose discharge pipe or a pump sitting on silt can cause noise and wear.

Smell from the basin

A sour odor can mean stagnant water, a missing lid or a basin that was never sealed.

Local note

Where Solon says the pit water cannot go

Solon's sewer code says sanitary sewers are not meant to carry storm, surface or ground water, and that no foundation drains may be tied into the sanitary sewer. The city also does dye testing in downspouts, driveways and area drains to find rain water entering the sanitary system. Those tests exist to find rain and groundwater entering the sanitary system.

We discharge the sump line to the outside, away from the foundation. The city's rule on exactly where it may discharge is not something we quote, so ask the Solon Building Department about your address before you change the route. That one phone call can save you a correction later.

Backups, battery power and new installs

Storms that fill pits also cause power cuts, which is when a plug-in pump is useless. A battery backup pump or a water-powered backup keeps the pit emptying through an outage. Ask us whether a backup suits your basement, since pit size and runtime matter.

For a new install, we size the pump to the pit and the water it has to move, set the check valve and run the discharge line away from the house. If water still gets in beyond what a pump can handle, read our page on basement waterproofing. For a flooded floor drain you might also need drain cleaning.
