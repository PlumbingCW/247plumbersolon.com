How to Waterproof a Basement in Solon, OH

Waterproofing a basement starts outside, with gutters, downspouts and grading, and ends inside with a drain and sump system if the water still gets in. Here is how to find the source of your water, which fixes work in which order, and what Solon homeowners should check before spending money.

Find where the water comes from before you fix anything

Basement water has only a few entry points. It seeps through the floor and wall joint, drips through cracks in the wall, comes up through the floor slab, or backs up from a drain. Each one has a different cure, and a fix aimed at the wrong one is money wasted.

Run a simple test. Tape a square of plastic wrap tight against a damp wall for two days. Moisture on the room side of the plastic means water is leaking through. Moisture trapped under the plastic means humid indoor air is condensing on a cool wall, and no waterproofing will cure that. A dehumidifier will.

Then watch the timing. Water that appears during or just after rain points to the outside of the house. Water that shows up on a dry day suggests a leaking pipe, a failed sump pump, or a high water table. Write down the date and the weather each time you see it.

The order that saves the most money

Start with the cheapest fix that stops the water, and move inward only when you must.

  • Extend the downspouts.. Water dumped beside the foundation soaks straight down the wall. Run each downspout several feet out onto a splash block or a buried pipe that leads away from the house.
  • Clean and reslope the gutters.. Clogged gutters overflow right at the wall. Clear them each fall and check that they pitch toward the downspouts.
  • Fix the grading.. The soil next to the house should fall away from the wall. Add compacted soil where it has settled, and keep mulch and flower beds from trapping water against the foundation.
  • Seal visible cracks.. A small crack in a poured wall can be injected from inside with a flexible sealer. Do this only after the outside water is under control, or the crack may reopen.
  • Add an interior drain and sump.. If water still comes in, a drain channel along the footing and a sump pump give it a path out. This is the most dependable fix for a basement that floods in heavy rain.

What interior waterproofing actually involves

An interior system cuts a trench along the inside edge of the slab, removes a strip of concrete, and lays a perforated drain pipe in gravel. Water that reaches the footing flows into the pipe and runs to a sump basin. A pump then lifts it out and away from the house.

This approach does not stop water from touching the outside of the wall. It gives that water a managed place to go, so it does not rise over the floor edge. That is why it works well for the common problem of seepage at the wall and floor joint, and why it is the usual answer when outdoor fixes are not enough.

Paint-on coatings and sealers are a different story. A coating can slow damp-looking walls and hide light efflorescence, which is the white powder left by mineral salts. It cannot hold back real water pressure from behind the wall, and it often peels. If you see standing water, a coating is not the answer.

Local note

Solon rules that shape a basement drain job

Solon's municipal code, in the sewer chapter, says foundation drains shall not be tied into the sanitary sewer. The city also runs dye testing to find places where rain or groundwater improperly enters the sanitary system. For you, this means the footing drain and sump discharge must not be hooked to the sewer line, and any plumber you hire should tell you where the pump will discharge.

The city's stormwater page also encourages downspout disconnection and rain barrels, and notes that some projects may need a permit. We did not find a published Solon rule that spells out where a sump pump must discharge, so ask the city Building Department before you finish the plan. Before any outdoor digging, dial 811 at least two working days ahead. The call is free.

The role of the sump pump

A sump pump sits in a basin below the floor level and switches on when the water rises. It is the heart of an interior system, so the pump, the float switch and the discharge line all matter. A stuck float is one of the most common reasons a basement floods during a storm.

Test the pump at the start of spring and again in fall. Pour a bucket of water into the basin and watch it start, empty the basin, and shut off. If it hums but does not move water, or it cycles constantly on dry days, it needs attention. Our sump pump repair service covers float, check valve and discharge line problems.

A battery backup is worth a conversation. Storms often cut power at the exact moment the pump is working hardest. A backup pump runs on a deep cycle battery for a few hours and keeps the basin from topping over.

Dealing with a flood while you wait

If water is already coming in, safety comes first. Do not walk through standing water if the furnace, outlets or the electrical panel are in the basement, because the water may be energized. Shut the power off at the breaker only if you can reach the panel without stepping in water.

Move what you can to higher ground, and if the water is clean rainwater, start removing it with a wet vac or a pump. If the water smells like sewage, treat it as contaminated, wear gloves and boots, and call right away.

Pictures help your plumber

Take photos of the wet area, the sump basin, and any stains on the wall with the date. Photos taken in the middle of a storm show exactly where water enters, which is hard to reproduce on a dry day.
